Humanoid robots developed in China are showcasing skills such as dancing, running and coordinated movement—bringing together advances in robotics engineering, motion control and AI.

Here are six ideas that help me understand their past, present and possible future:

![Humanoid robot building blocks: a human-like body, sensors, an AI brain, and motion control. The infographic also compares VLA and WAM and shows examples of humanoid products.]({{ '/assets/blog/ai-jargon-101/humanoid-robot-building-blocks.jpg' | relative_url }})

## 1. Pioneers such as Boston Dynamics laid the foundations.

Their work pushed robot mobility, balance and whole-body control forward. Those breakthroughs helped create the foundation for today’s humanoids—and Boston Dynamics continues to develop that future.

## 2. Different bodies, shared building blocks.

A robot vacuum, a four-legged inspection robot, a humanoid—and even an autonomous car—share the same basic formula:

**Body + Sensors + “Brain” + Motion control**

The body performs the task. Sensors detect what is happening. The “brain” chooses actions. Motion control turns those decisions into movement.

An autonomous car follows this same principle: its body carries people or goods, sensors observe the road, software makes driving decisions, and control systems steer, accelerate and brake. The architecture is similar, even though the hardware and software are designed for different jobs.

## 3. The “brain” is evolving.

VLA—Vision-Language-Action—is a prominent approach today: connect what the robot sees and what you ask with an action.

WAM—World Action Model—is an emerging direction: connect predicted outcomes with the actions that produce them. It’s a promising development, although it won’t necessarily replace every VLA or controller.

## 4. Skills can travel between robots.

This reminds me of downloading skills in The Matrix. Boston Dynamics says a task learned by one Atlas can be deployed across its Atlas fleet. Figure has also demonstrated multiple humanoids operating with the same learned AI policy.

That means compatible robots don’t need to learn every skill individually from scratch. But it isn’t instant mastery of anything: new environments, hardware differences and safety requirements can still call for adaptation and testing.

## 5. LLMs have abundant internet data. WAMs need more physical experience.

LLMs learn from vast amounts of human-created content on the internet, including text and code. WAMs have much less training data that connects what a robot observes, the actions it takes, and what happens next—including contact, force and failure.

Human videos and simulation can help fill the gap, but they don’t capture every detail of real-world interaction. Collecting diverse, high-quality physical experience remains a major bottleneck.

## 6. Humanoids could offer broader usefulness.

My view is that a human-like body has a strong advantage in a world built around people: our stairs, tools, kitchens and workstations.

Specialized forms will still make sense, though. A robot vacuum doesn’t need legs to clean a floor efficiently.
